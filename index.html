El Salvador · 1995-2020 · A visual essay

First Breaths

In 2000 El Salvador's Ministry of Health set out to put a person trained in neonatal resuscitation at every hospital birth. Twenty years and 17,695 trainings later, neonatal mortality has fallen by more than half. How much of that is the training? This essay follows the numbers, including the ones that do not cooperate.

Felipe H. Rodríguez Villalta · September 2026 · Data: Ministry of Health of El Salvador, UN IGME, WHO, World Bank

The workforce the program set out to train

Each square stands for ten of the roughly 4,500 doctors, nurses and therapists who attended births in the 28 public delivery hospitals. Filled squares: trained by the end of 2004.

Coverage of eligible staff reached 5 percent in 2000, 12 in 2001, 38 in 2002, 60 in 2003 and 76 in 2004. Source: Comité Nacional de Reanimación Neonatal, as reported at the Pediatric Academic Societies meeting, 2005.

About one newborn in ten needs help to start breathing. Three to six in a hundred need air pushed into their lungs with a bag and a mask, within the first minute, by someone who knows how. When that does not happen, the death is recorded as birth asphyxia. Around the turn of the century such deaths were about a quarter of all neonatal deaths in the world.

The skills that prevent most of them fit in a two-day course. That course, the Neonatal Resuscitation Program of the American Academy of Pediatrics, is what the Ministry of Health decided in 1999 to teach to everyone who attends a birth in the public hospitals. This essay is about what happened next, and about a harder question: how anyone can tell whether a program like this worked when the only record is one national series and a start date.

One thing to keep in view throughout. The 28 hospitals belong to the Ministry of Health, on which about four fifths of the population depends. The families whose newborns are most at risk, poor, rural, far from a referral hospital, are the families who give birth there, and the program selected no one: the goal was a trained person at every delivery. A cheap, universal intervention in the public system is an instrument of equity as much as of survival. It also has an edge. In 2000 a substantial share of births, between a tenth and a third depending on how attendance by trained midwives is counted, took place outside a facility, and those births were beyond the reach of anything that happens in a delivery room. Keep them in mind; the data on this page cannot see them.

Chapter 1Four years to train a country

A committee, a curriculum and 28 hospitals. The roll-out was fast, complete, and then it never really stopped.

Providers trained

Show the numbers
2000In 1999 the Ministry of Health, the Salvadoran paediatric association and the American Academy of Pediatrics formed a National Neonatal Resuscitation Committee. The first national instructor course ran in 2000. That year 239 providers were trained: five percent of the workforce.
2002Instructors then ran courses hospital by hospital. By the end of 2002 they had trained 1,699 providers, 38 percent of the workforce, with the Pan American Health Organization organising the instructor courses and a USAID project paying for implementation.
2004By 2004, 3,415 providers, three quarters of the workforce. Every public hospital with a delivery room now had trained staff. The stated goal was operational: at least one person trained in resuscitation present at every delivery.
2005 to 2019The program did not stop. New staff, rotating residents and refresher courses kept the annual count between 560 and 1,349 for fifteen more years. By 2019 it had delivered 17,695 trainings, four times the size of the workforce. Studies of skill retention say that is roughly what it takes: simulated performance after a one-day course decays within months.
2020The pandemic closed the courses. The trained workforce stayed in place, which matters for how the last year should be read.

Chapter 2The decline, and the trap

The Ministry's series fell by two thirds. Reading that as the program's effect is the trap this essay is about.

Neonatal mortality, hospital births

Deaths per 1,000 live births

Show the numbers
1995 to 2020The Ministry of Health's neonatal mortality rate for hospital births fell from 17 deaths per 1,000 live births in 1995 to 5 in 2020. The study that this essay revisits read that fall, and a regression of the rate on a "training years" indicator, as evidence that the program worked.
Before the programLook at the five years before 2000. The rate was already falling seven percent a year. Whatever was driving that, it was not resuscitation training, because there was none.
The extrapolated trendExtend the 1995-1999 trend forward and it lies below what happened. After 2000 the decline continued at about five percent a year; it did not accelerate. The study's regression coefficient, minus 0.57 log points, is what a falling series produces when you compare five early years with twenty later ones. It measures the trend.
2009Then something happens. In one year neonatal mortality drops 39 percent. Break the series down by age at death and the picture changes: deaths at 7 to 27 days fall 76 percent, deaths after the first month fall 34 percent, first-week deaths fall 12 percent. Deaths that happen after a newborn leaves the hospital stopped being captured. The Ministry's online mortality system starts in 2009, the same year as a health reform. This is a change in measurement, not in risk.
After 2009The late and post-neonatal rates drift back up as the new system matures; the first-week rate, the one least affected by what happens after discharge, is flat. The clean window for a before-and-after comparison is 1995 to 2008, and even there the answer is negative: the program did not bend a trend that was already steep.

Chapter 3Synthetic El Salvador

If the trend cannot be the counterfactual, the region can. A weighted mix of countries that matched El Salvador's 1990s path stands in for the El Salvador that never trained anyone.

Neonatal mortality, national estimates

Deaths per 1,000 live births, UN IGME 2021

Show the numbers
National estimatesThe UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation produces comparable national series for every country, all births included. For El Salvador the neonatal rate fell from 23 per 1,000 in 1990 to 15 in 2000 and 6.5 in 2019.
The regionNeonatal mortality was falling everywhere else in Latin America and the Caribbean too. Here are the 24 other countries. El Salvador's decline in the 1990s, before the program, was already faster than the regional average, which is exactly why the regional average is the wrong comparison.
The synthetic controlThe synthetic control method finds the mix of countries that reproduces El Salvador's 1990-1999 path: Peru (weight 0.51), Guyana (0.19), Chile (0.13), Honduras (0.08), Belize (0.06) and Venezuela (0.02). The match through 1999 is exact to three decimals. Nothing after 1999 is used to build it.
After 2000The two lines separate slowly. The gap is 4 percent during the roll-out, 10 percent in 2005-2009 and 19 percent in 2010-2019: 13 percent over two decades, or 1.8 deaths per 1,000 live births by 2019. In several donor countries individual hospitals adopted the same training, so if anything this understates the difference between training and no training.
Is that unusual?Run the same exercise for every country as if it had been the one treated. Seven of the 24 show larger gaps than El Salvador. On the standard test El Salvador ranks 9th of 25 (p = 0.36); on the augmented estimator and on the 2024 data vintage, p = 0.12. The gap is consistent with the program and with every other change in newborn care in El Salvador after 2000. Suggestive, not conclusive.

Chapter 4Where the roll-out shows up

A resuscitation program cannot do just anything. It acts in the first minutes of life, on one cause of death. That is a testable prediction.

Hospital mortality by age at death

Index, 2000 = 100

Show the numbers
By age at deathIf the training worked, it should show among first-week deaths, not at 7 to 27 days, and not after the first month, when infants die of infection and malformation. During the roll-out, 2000 to 2004, the hospital series do exactly that: first-week mortality fell 26 percent, mortality at 7 to 27 days rose 13 percent, post-neonatal mortality fell 17 percent. The first-week share of neonatal deaths dropped from 57 to 47 percent. The late-neonatal decline came afterwards, with neonatal care units and infection management.
By causeThe hospital reporting forms add the cause. Between 1999 and 2004 deaths attributed to birth asphyxia fell 43 percent, from 1.22 to 0.70 per 1,000, while all other causes fell 13 percent. The difference, about half a death per 1,000 live births, is the upper bound on what the roll-out did. Six annual points, no comparison group: a bound, not an estimate.
A control outcomeAcross countries, the ratio of neonatal to post-neonatal mortality nets out everything that moves all infant deaths: income, fertility, sanitation, vaccines, the reform, the reporting system. In the region the ratio rose after 2000, because neonatal deaths are the hard ones. In El Salvador it stayed flat. Against its synthetic control El Salvador ranks 5th of 25 (p = 0.20). The set of competing explanations shrinks to things that act on newborns specifically.

Chapter 5The region, and the bill

El Salvador had the largest proportional decline in neonatal mortality in Latin America between 2000 and 2019. What the program can claim of it is bounded, and the bound is enough to pay for it many times over.

Change in the neonatal mortality rate, 2000 to 2019

UN IGME 2021 national estimates, 25 countries

Show the numbers

El Salvador's rate fell 57 percent, just ahead of Peru; the average of the other 24 countries was 37 percent. The rate was also falling fast in the 1990s, 31 percent against a regional 22, and the edge persisted in the 2000s and the 2010s. A comparison with the regional average would credit the program with about twice what the synthetic control does, and half of the difference would be El Salvador's own trajectory.

The evidence does not yield a point estimate with a defensible confidence interval, so the essay does not invent one. It yields bounds. At the upper bound, half a death per 1,000 institutional births, the program averted 47 to 60 deaths a year in the early 2000s; at the 30 percent reduction in intrapartum-related deaths that the trial literature supports, 33 to 42. On the cost side, 885 trainings a year at 100 to 300 dollars each, plus a committee, coordination and delivery-room equipment, come to between 130,000 and 385,000 dollars a year.

Cost per disability-adjusted life year averted, in US dollars, under alternative effect sizes; thirty discounted DALYs per neonatal death averted; unit costs from the published range for NRP and Helping Babies Breathe courses. El Salvador's GDP per capita in 2019 was about 4,100 dollars, the usual threshold for "cost-effective" in the region's health technology assessments.

It would take an effect an order of magnitude smaller than the smallest scenario above, or costs several times higher than any reported for these courses, to make the program a poor use of money. That is a stronger policy statement than any point estimate these data could support.

Who was not in the roomThe question the data cannot answer

Everything above concerns births in hospitals. In 2000, somewhere between a tenth and a third of El Salvador's births took place outside a facility, and by 2010 very few did. Those births belonged, almost certainly, to the poorest households and the most remote communities, and for them a program that acts in delivery rooms did nothing until institutional delivery caught up with them. A hospital-based program can lower the national average and, for as long as institutional delivery is incomplete, widen the gap between families inside the system and families outside it. Whether the decline documented here was shared across wealth quintiles and regions, or concentrated among those already inside, is not a question the Ministry series or the UN IGME estimates can answer. The household surveys can, and it should be asked before the program is held up as a model.

What would settle itThree files that exist, or existed

The hospital panel

The study protocols describe six-month periods per hospital before and after each hospital's training, with deaths by cause and birth weight. Because the roll-out reached the 28 hospitals in sequence over four years, that file supports a staggered difference-in-differences and an event study. It would replace everything on this page.

Vital registration

El Salvador reports registered deaths by ICD-10 cause and age in days, including 0 to 6 and 7 to 27 days, to the WHO Mortality Database. Asphyxia against malformations and extreme prematurity, before and after 2000, nationally and annually, against other countries in the same database. Public, and free.

Birth histories

The FESAL surveys of 1998, 2002-03 and 2008 and the 2014 MICS recorded every birth with its place of delivery. Neonatal mortality among facility births relative to home births, before and after 2000, is a difference the program should produce and nothing else in the reform period should; the same surveys carry a wealth index and say who shared in the decline.

Methods in brief

Interrupted time series: segmented regression of log neonatal mortality with a level and slope change in 2000, estimated on 1995-2008 (before the reporting change) with Newey-West and Prais-Winsten errors and placebo interruption dates. Synthetic control: Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller (2010) on log neonatal mortality from UN IGME, 24 Latin American and Caribbean donors, every pre-period year 1990-1999 as a predictor; checked with a covariate-based specification, the ridge-augmented estimator of Ben-Michael, Feller and Rothstein (2021), estimates in levels, a restricted donor pool and the 2024 vintage; inference by in-space permutation (rank of El Salvador among the 25 units on the ratio of post- to pre-period fit and on the mean gap), leave-one-out and an in-time placebo in 1995. Control outcome: the same method on the log ratio of neonatal to post-neonatal mortality. Composition: Ministry rates by age at death and the 1999-2004 cause-specific series from the hospital reporting forms, presented as bounds. Cost-effectiveness: scenario grid over effect sizes and unit costs, thirty discounted DALYs per death averted.
